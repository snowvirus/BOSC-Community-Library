## Chosen License: Apache License 2.0

For the BOSC Community Library, I chose the Apache License 2.0 because it fits the kind of public education project I am building as a Bachelor of Science in Software Engineering student at Bugema University. The project is not meant to be a private system owned by one person. It is meant to be a shared community library where schools, teachers, learners, and other developers can use the resources, improve them, and still understand their legal responsibilities.

In open source software, a license is not just a formality. It tells people what they are allowed to do with the project and what limits they must respect. I considered permissive licenses such as MIT and stronger copyleft licenses such as GPL. MIT is simple and popular, but it is very short and does not clearly address patents. GPL protects openness by requiring derivative works to remain under similar terms, but for this project I felt it could discourage some institutions or companies from participating. A public-sector library should be easy to adopt by schools, government offices, non-profit organizations, and even private service providers who may want to offer hosting or support.

Apache 2.0 is useful because it gives broad permission to use, copy, modify, and distribute the project, including for commercial purposes. This is important for a public-sector project because government adoption usually requires legal clarity. If the Ministry of Education or a school district wanted to use the BOSC Community Library, they would not need to negotiate a separate license before testing or adapting it. They can inspect the repository, reuse the work, and build on it as long as they follow the license conditions.

The biggest advantage of Apache 2.0 over MIT in this case is the patent grant. Apache 2.0 states that contributors grant users a license to any relevant patent claims connected to their contributions. It also has a patent termination clause, meaning a person or company that starts certain patent litigation against the project can lose the patent protection given by the license. From my research, this is one reason Apache 2.0 is common in serious software projects where organizations want more legal confidence than a very simple license provides.

Trademark protection is also important. Apache 2.0 does not automatically give other people the right to use the project name, logo, or identity as if they represent the official maintainers. For BOSC Community Library, this matters because users should be able to know which repository is the official one. A company may fork the project, but it should not mislead schools or learners into thinking its version is the official community version.

The license also allows a commercial entity to create a paid version of the library. For example, a company could offer hosted access, technical support, training, or extra features. That is allowed under Apache 2.0, but the company must still preserve the original license notices and cannot claim ownership of the original open source work. I think this is a fair balance. The community keeps the free core project, while service providers can earn money by adding real value. For public institutions, this also reduces vendor lock-in because the original repository remains available even if one paid provider becomes too expensive.
